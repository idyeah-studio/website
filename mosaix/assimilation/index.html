Employee onboarding · the experience

Assimilation.

The whole six months in one tab. Eleven wireframes.

Wireframes, not visual design. Nothing about type, color or craft is decided here.

The idea underneath

Everything in six months is one list of items.

A compliance module, a culture video, a coffee with the engineering partner, a Figma course, the month-six assessment. One kind of thing: a piece of work with an owner, a due date, and a way to do it. They differ in type, phase and when they unlock, not in where they live. Every row in every frame below is one of these.

Seven types, six statuses, one table. Four sub-tabs sit on top of it: Now is the list filtered to what is due. Plan is the whole table grouped by phase. Resources is every attached file, browsable on its own. People is the contacts.

Numbered notes sit under each frame.

Anything in lilac is the AI layer. It is additive. Remove all of it and every screen still works, with nothing left blank and nothing to rearrange.

Notes on W1
  1. Grouped by urgency, not by type. A new hire thinks in deadlines, not content types. Type is a label on the row, never a grouping.
  2. Minutes on every row. It is what lets someone with fifteen free minutes pick something rather than nothing.
  3. "Waiting on someone" is its own group. When the manager has not acted, the item is visible, dated, blameless and chaseable. Without this state the screen either lies or goes blank in week one.
  4. "Available to start now" is the working-ahead group. Future items with nothing blocking them, offered to anyone with a quiet afternoon. Pulling one forward changes its position, not its due date, so nothing ever looks overdue that is not. This works whether or not items have prerequisites.
  5. AI sits at the end of the tab row, not in the list. It appears only when it has something worth saying, and opens a small overlay rather than pushing into the day's work. See W11.
  6. Two rows carry an AI note, and the rest do not. These are things the task description cannot know: which system it runs through, when someone is actually free. Scarcity is what makes them worth reading. If every row had one, none would.
  7. The AI layer is additive. It rides inside the meta line that already flexes, so removing every lilac element leaves the row exactly as it was. No reserved space, no empty state, nothing to rearrange.
  8. The overdue item gets no encouragement copy. It states the days late and offers two exits: do it, or ask for more time. The extension request is what keeps completion data honest.
  9. Two readings of progress, deliberately. The butterfly top right carries the feeling. The band carries the count, the bar and the per-phase split. One is for the heart, one is for planning the week.
  10. The primary button verb changes with type. Start, Open, Book a slot, Do it now. Same component, one field driving the label.

Notes on W5
  1. One table, every kind of thing. Compliance, culture, people, courses and the assessment sit in a single list, told apart by a Type column rather than by which screen they live on.
  2. Grouped by phase, then chronological inside it. Not by type. The employee's question is "when", never "what category".
  3. The Builds column carries the skills view. Filtering by it answers "what am I doing about Accessibility" without needing a separate screen for it.
  4. Completion carries its date and its proof. "Complete · Feb 12 · Certificate" in a cell costs nothing now and is the record HR needs later.
  5. The month strip is the arc at a glance. Six columns, item count and completion per month, clickable to filter the table below. It gives the shape of the six months without a second view to build and keep in sync.
  6. Deadline clustering is the reason it earns its place. "3 due in one week" on Month 2 is invisible in a table and obvious here. Compliance gets scheduled in clumps and nobody notices until they are on top of you.
  7. Filters are load-bearing. Forty-six rows is unmanageable without them. "Hide completed" is the one used most by month four.
  8. Print and Export sit in the header. She wants the plan for her own records, HR wants the completion file.
  9. One AI note sits in a table cell. Same layer, same lilac, inside the existing Item cell rather than a column of its own. A tip column would have broken the rule that the layer lifts off cleanly.
  10. Truncation rows are shown as italic counts. In the build these are ordinary rows. They are collapsed here only to keep the wireframe readable.
  11. Locked Build items are shown in full from day one. It makes the six months concrete rather than a rumor, at the cost of a longer table in the first weeks. Worth the trade.

Notes on W6
  1. One panel, four sections, every type. What this is · How to do it · Resources · Who can help. An engineer builds it once. The type field decides what fills each section and what the primary button says.
  2. "How to complete it" is a numbered list, always. So nobody has to guess when they are finished.

Notes on W10
  1. One filter, no new screen. Working ahead is a view of the table that already exists. "Only what I could do today" is the whole feature.
  2. The strip answers where the slack is. Two available in Month 3, two in Month 4. She can see which month she is eating into before she commits an afternoon to it.
  3. The "why it is available" column is the honest part. It says out loud whether something is open because nothing blocks it, or because a prerequisite has cleared. The same column, inverted, explains why the locked ones are locked. That is the piece most tools leave the employee to guess.
  4. Being ahead is measured in items, not percentages. "Three items ahead of where the plan expects you" is a sentence someone can feel. Thirty-nine percent is not.
  5. Pulling forward does not move the due date. The deadline stays where it was. Only the position in the list changes, so nothing can accidentally read as overdue.
  6. Some things genuinely cannot move. The assessment has a fixed window, and a compliance item tied to a policy version may have an earliest date as well as a due date. Those stay locked and say why.
  7. Dependencies are authored once, per plan. Where nothing blocks anything, this works exactly as drawn. Where a course assumes another, that pairing is set when the plan is built and the screen reads it. Hard-gating whole phases would shrink this to nothing, which is the one version worth avoiding.

  1. It is an action, not a feed. AI sits at the end of the tab row and only appears when there is something to say. On a quiet week it is simply not there, and the screen is unchanged.

  3. Every suggestion can be acted on in one move. A suggestion she cannot act on without navigating somewhere is just a notification.
  4. Each one states its reason. The second line is evidence, not decoration. Without it she stops reading them by month two.
  5. Two at a time, not ten. If there are more than a handful, the threshold for saying something is set too low.
  6. Dismissing works by kind, not by instance. Three ignored pace readings stop the pace readings. The product should take the hint.
